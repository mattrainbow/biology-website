--- title: Evolution has used the same bones to build all these different mammalian forelimbs summary: Watch one set of bones reshape into nine mammal forelimbs, from a human arm to a bat's wing, a whale's flipper and a horse's hoof. scripts: data.js, skeleton.js, renderer.js, interactive.js ---

A human hand, a bat’s wing, a whale’s flipper and a horse’s hoof look nothing alike, yet each is built from the same set of bones: one upper-arm bone, two forearm bones, a cluster of wrist bones and five fingers or their remnants. Watch the limbs reshape into one another and you can follow every bone from animal to animal as it lengthens, shrinks or fuses, but is never swapped for something new. That shared blueprint, inherited from a common ancestor, is some of the clearest evidence that these mammals are related.

Why the match matters

Structures that share an underlying plan because they were inherited from a common ancestor are called homologous. A wing, a flipper, a hoof and a hand do very different jobs, yet all follow the same layout: one upper-arm bone, two forearm bones, a cluster of wrist bones, then five rays of palm and finger bones.

Nothing about flying, swimming or galloping requires that layout. An engineer designing a wing or a flipper from scratch would not build it out of a hand. The shared pattern makes sense as inheritance: all nine animals are mammals, descended from ancestors that already had it.

Evolution changed the limbs mostly by changing how long and thick each bone grows, and by letting some bones shrink away. The horse is the clearest case: it still carries the remnants of fingers II and IV as splint bones. The pattern itself goes back to the first four-limbed animals, more than 350 million years ago.
